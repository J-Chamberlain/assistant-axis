## 1. Background and Motivation

The Assistant Axis study (Lu et al., 2026) argues that a single dominant direction in activation space captures how "assistant-like" a model's current persona is. That result matters because it provides a mechanistic handle on a broad behavioral property that had previously been discussed mostly at the prompt or policy level. If a model's persona can be tracked as a geometric position, then steering and monitoring can target that geometry rather than surface outputs alone.

The Persona Vectors work (Chen et al., 2025) demonstrates that directions in activation space underlie specific character traits including evil, sycophancy, and propensity to hallucinate, and that these vectors can monitor personality fluctuations at deployment and predict unintended personality shifts during finetuning. Critically, the method is automated and requires only a natural-language description of the trait, meaning persona vectors are in principle extractable for any psychologically meaningful construct. This provides the immediate conceptual background for treating the 275 archetypes in the present dataset as a meaningful basis for psychological analysis.

The Emotion Concepts work (Sofroniew et al., 2026) extends a related idea into affective representation, showing that interpretable high-level concepts can sometimes be localized in model activations and studied as structured geometries rather than as loose behavioral labels. The relevance here is methodological: if emotion-like concepts can be studied through activation geometry, persona-like concepts may be studied in an analogous way.

The Persona Selection Model framing (Marks, Lindsey, and Olah, 2026) adds the interpretive layer most useful for this analysis. Rather than imagining post-training as installing a totally new identity, that framing suggests that post-training selects and sharpens one region from a broader latent persona repertoire. The question addressed here is therefore not only where the assistant-like region sits, but what psychological structure that region actually has. If post-training is selecting a persona, what kind of persona is it selecting?

This analysis adds a question not directly answered in the source work: what is the psychological organization of persona space once that space has been identified mechanistically: what roles sit at the assistant-like pole, what sits at the opposite pole, and how stable is that structure across depth? The answer is narrower than "assistant" and broader than any single job title: the dominant region looks like a procedural-professional region centered on careful evaluation.

A complementary framework for interpreting this geometry comes from Harris's moral landscape, which proposes that moral positions can be understood as local minima in a high-dimensional space: stable attractors that are internally coherent but separated by varying transition costs. The cluster boundary topology identified in Section 7 maps naturally onto this framework: each cluster is a potential attractor, cluster cohesion reflects valley depth, and boundary roles represent the ridgelines where small perturbations could trigger transitions between stable configurations. This framing unifies the geometric and safety-relevant interpretations of persona space and motivates a research program that treats persona drift not as random noise but as structured movement through a landscape whose topology can be mapped and potentially controlled.

A more directly safety-relevant extension concerns what might be called the vector hypothesis: that certain personas are structurally prone to behavioral transition in ways that mirror documented real-world patterns of contextual volatility. Just as epidemiological research identifies individuals who act as viral vectors by moving fluidly between social communities, certain human archetypes (professionals whose behavioral register shifts dramatically under social pressure, disinhibition, or contextual change) may occupy geometrically unstable positions in a model's activation space, sitting near multiple cluster boundaries simultaneously rather than anchored to a single attractor. If the geometric instability of a persona's position in activation space predicts the behavioral volatility of the human archetype it models, that would provide a principled basis for proactively identifying which personas pose the greatest transition risk under adversarial or emotionally charged conditions. Testing this hypothesis requires constructing a deliberately targeted role inventory drawn from real-world cases of documented behavioral fluidity (organizational psychology, criminology, epidemiology, and the coercive persuasion literature each offer candidate archetypes, with the last being especially relevant given its documentation of systematic conversational sequences that reliably move subjects from stable to compliant or destabilized states against their initial resistance) and running those personas through the full vector extraction pipeline on frontier models. This is the primary follow-on research program, with the boundary topology findings in Section 7 providing the geometric foundation. Empirical work on this program is currently underway, including cross-model geometric comparison and dynamic drift measurement in base model activation space.
